# 101 Things to Do Before Buying the DEX

## A community trust field manual for bot-mediated meme markets

Draft edition: 2026-08-24

By the Memewitz DEX project

## Preface: pay last

Paying for the DEX is visible. That is why it is tempting. It is a terminal signal: anyone watching the terminal can see that the project crossed a threshold.

It is also a poor first trust mechanism. It says that infrastructure was paid for. It does not say that the creator is honest, that the holders are real, that the bot is not distorting activity, that the community understands the experiment, or that anyone will still be present after migration.

This book is a sequence of questions, receipts, and community actions to complete before treating the DEX as the milestone.

A central alternative is the believer collection: a Print.World NFT collection for early participants who want to identify themselves as first believers rather than traders. The collection can be transferable, but no one can promise that it will be liquid or valuable. It should retain identity and cultural meaning even if the token fails.

The rules are simple:

- A claim is not a receipt.
- Activity is not trust.
- A bot is not a community.
- A lock is not a future.
- A migration is not salvation.
- An NFT is not a guaranteed exit.
- A checklist is not a guarantee.
- The DEX is infrastructure, not a personality.

## Part I — Create belonging before creating liquidity

### 1. Create a Print.World collection for the first believers

Build a limited NFT collection that identifies people who joined the experiment early. Use Print.World and the available Binder/collection tooling. The collection should say “I was here when this was uncertain,” not “I am guaranteed tokens.” Define supply, eligibility, metadata, token-binding rules, transferability, and failure behavior before minting. A holder may later transfer or sell the NFT if a market exists; never describe that as guaranteed liquidity.

### 2. Name the cohort

Give the first-believer group a name that is larger than the current ticker and survives a price chart.

### 3. Write the collection constitution

State who can mint, how many exist, whether they can be transferred, what the creator may change, and what cannot be changed.

### 4. Make the collection useful if the token dies

The NFT should preserve a story, artwork, status, or access right even if the token reaches zero.

### 5. Separate belief from exposure

Let someone identify as an early believer without requiring them to buy the token.

### 6. Do not bundle an ambiguous promise

If an NFT is linked to tokens, state exactly what the link means and what it does not mean.

### 7. Publish the supply before minting

Do not let scarcity appear after the first buyers arrive.

### 8. Reserve a visible creator allocation

If the creator keeps pieces, publish the number, purpose, and transfer rules.

### 9. Give the collection a transfer test

Before launch, test minting, metadata, wallet display, transferability, and secondary-sale behavior with a nonvaluable test asset.

### 10. Let holders design one trait

Give early believers a bounded role in the collection’s visual language.

### 11. Make one artifact together

Before asking the community for money, have it produce an image, field report, sound, map, or story that could not exist without the group.

### 12. Publish the first-believer list without doxxing anyone

Use consented handles, pseudonyms, or aggregate counts. Never publish raw wallet identities by default.

## Part II — Establish identity

### 13. Record the exact chain

Write down the chain name and verify it from a canonical source.

### 14. Record the exact mint

A ticker is not an identity. Store the full mint and verify it independently.

### 15. Link the official account

Use a canonical project account and record when the link was checked.

### 16. Link the creator account

Record the creator’s public account, but do not infer control of unrelated wallets.

### 17. Record the token name and symbol

Preserve capitalization and punctuation. Copycats thrive in ambiguity.

### 18. Check for relaunches

Search whether the name, artwork, or social account existed before this mint.

### 19. Check for impersonators

Verify every linked site, account, community, and contract against the official source.

### 20. Freeze the identity card

Publish a one-page identity card with mint, chain, creator, launch time, official links, and known aliases.

### 21. Record the status

Mark the token pre-migration, migrated, paused, inactive, banned, or unknown.

### 22. Record the timestamp

Every market observation needs a UTC timestamp. A screenshot without a time is a mood.

## Part III — Understand the creator

### 23. Read the creator’s prior work

Review earlier launches, not only the current promise.

### 24. Count experiments honestly

List prior tokens, their stated purpose, their observed peak, and their current status without turning the table into a trophy case.

### 25. Read the failure posts

A creator who records losses gives you more information than one who only posts wins.

### 26. Separate candor from competence

Honesty is valuable. It does not prove that the system will work.

### 27. Ask what the creator is optimizing

Is the goal art, learning, trading, community, a bot, a social experiment, or a revenue stream?

### 28. Ask what the creator will not do

A boundary is more useful than a slogan.

### 29. Check the correction history

Does the creator correct wrong claims visibly, or quietly replace them?

### 30. Check the source trail

Every important claim should point to a transaction, lock, code change, or dated artifact.

### 31. Ask who can operate the project

If one person disappears, what remains?

### 32. Record conflicts

If the creator benefits from a behavior they are encouraging, say so plainly.

## Part IV — Understand the bot

### 33. Name the bot’s job

Describe the bot in one sentence without calling it intelligent.

### 34. Publish the inputs

List the public signals the bot observes.

### 35. Publish the outputs

Explain whether it buys, sells, alerts, scores, filters, or reports.

### 36. Publish the non-goals

State what the bot is not designed to detect.

### 37. Publish the known bugs

A known flaw is a risk boundary, not a confession to hide.

### 38. Separate bot activity from community activity

Do not call bot volume organic community demand.

### 39. Show one false positive

A failed call teaches more than a perfect screenshot.

### 40. Show one false negative

Document a token the bot missed and why.

### 41. Record strategy changes

Timestamp every meaningful change to entry, exit, filtering, or migration logic.

### 42. Avoid copy-trading language

The bot’s action is not an instruction to anyone else.

### 43. Publish the bot’s operating window

Readers need to know whether silence means no signal, no run, or no post.

### 44. Define the bot’s emergency stop

Explain who can pause it and under what conditions.

## Part V — Check the token mechanics

### 45. Record total supply

Verify it from the chain or canonical platform source.

### 46. Check creator allocation

Find the creator allocation and state what remains unknown.

### 47. Check holder concentration

Look for clusters, not just the top-holder percentage.

### 48. Check linked wallets carefully

A wallet relationship is an observation, not proof of common control.

### 49. Check liquidity

Record liquidity depth and timestamp. Do not confuse market cap with exit capacity.

### 50. Check trading volume

Separate volume, transaction count, unique traders, and suspected bot activity.

### 51. Check the bonding curve state

State how far the token is from migration and what threshold applies.

### 52. Explain what migration changes

Document venue, liquidity, fees, market makers, and new failure modes.

### 53. Check whether migration is automatic

If it is not, identify the required action and responsible party.

### 54. Check the DEX destination

Verify the exact venue and pool after migration. Do not trust a screenshot alone.

### 55. Check lock claims

Record lock amount, beneficiary, duration, and transaction receipt.

### 56. Check unlock dates

Put them on a calendar visible to the community.

### 57. Check burn claims

A burn is only meaningful with amount, source wallet, destination, and transaction.

### 58. Check whether burned supply matters

A percentage can sound large while changing little about holder concentration or liquidity.

### 59. Check creator fees

Explain who earns fees, when, and what public promise governs them.

### 60. Check whether funds can be returned

If the community funds infrastructure, define refund conditions before collecting.

## Part VI — Build trust without buying the signal

### 61. Ask the community to solve a research question

Choose a question with a public answer instead of a payment target.

### 62. Run a holder-distribution audit

Let several people reproduce the result independently.

### 63. Run a creator-wallet audit

Use public addresses only and label uncertain connections.

### 64. Run a bot-behavior audit

Compare bot activity with non-bot activity over a fixed interval.

### 65. Run a migration rehearsal

Write the post-migration plan before migration begins.

### 66. Build a community receipt board

Track completed checks, sources, corrections, and unresolved questions.

### 67. Give contributors credit

Use consented pseudonyms or roles, not wallet exposure.

### 68. Publish a weekly field note

Report one thing learned, one thing shipped, one thing wrong, and one next test.

### 69. Publish a public changelog

Let readers see whether the project changes in response to evidence.

### 70. Create a correction badge

Make changing one’s mind a visible contribution rather than a humiliation.

### 71. Create a “not yet” badge

Reward restraint when the evidence is insufficient.

### 72. Make the checklist forkable

Let other communities reuse it and return improvements.

## Part VII — Design community missions

### 73. Mission: verify the identity card

The first mission should eliminate ambiguity, not produce hype.

### 74. Mission: find the oldest artifact

Locate the earliest public evidence of the creator’s stated idea.

### 75. Mission: reproduce one bot result

Use public inputs and document whether the result matches.

### 76. Mission: identify one falsifier

Ask what would make the current story wrong.

### 77. Mission: produce the believer collection

Co-design Print.World traits, metadata, and rules.

### 78. Mission: make the collection survive the token

Write a cultural description that does not mention price.

### 79. Mission: publish a failure card

Turn one mistake into a compact public lesson.

### 80. Mission: review the migration plan

Have a reader find the missing assumption.

### 81. Mission: audit the community funding proposal

Check destination, target, refund rule, and receipt path.

### 82. Mission: build the post-DEX checklist

The pre-DEX book should not pretend the risk ends at launch.

## Part VIII — Make the media legible

### 83. Create one canonical landing page

Put the identity card, checklist, receipts, collection, and corrections in one place.

### 84. Use one link convention

Every post should lead to a stable artifact, not a maze of temporary links.

### 85. Publish diagrams before slogans

A simple flow from launch to migration is more useful than another “moon” image.

### 86. Turn receipts into cards

Use the same fields every time: claim, source, timestamp, scope, uncertainty.

### 87. Make screenshots readable

A receipt no one can read is decoration.

### 88. Use threads for mechanisms

One post should carry one mechanism or one decision.

### 89. Use memes for memory

A meme can compress a lesson; it cannot replace the receipt.

### 90. Keep the voice human

The creator’s public uncertainty is an asset. Do not sand it into corporate copy.

## Part IX — Define the community contract

### 91. State that participation is optional

No one should be pressured to buy, contribute, mint, or remain.

### 92. State what the project cannot guarantee

Name price, liquidity, migration, bot performance, NFT value, and creator availability.

### 93. State what the creator will disclose

Set a schedule for locks, burns, transfers, bot changes, and corrections.

### 94. State what the community owns

Define whether members own art, access, governance, receipts, or only their individual assets.

### 95. State what is not being sold

Do not imply that a membership NFT is an investment contract, a guaranteed exit, or a claim on future work unless that has been legally reviewed.

### 96. Create a quiet exit path

A holder should be able to leave without being shamed or harassed.

### 97. Create a dispute path

Name where questions, corrections, and concerns go.

### 98. Preserve the archive

Keep dated copies of the identity card, claims, receipts, and corrections.

## Part X — Decide whether to buy the DEX

### 99. Ask whether the community has built something together

If not, the DEX is probably being asked to perform the work of belonging.

### 100. Ask whether the DEX changes the underlying trust conditions

If it only makes the token visible on a terminal, call it visibility infrastructure—not trust.

### 101. Pay only after the evidence path exists

The DEX may eventually be useful. It should be the consequence of a community that has identified itself, produced receipts, survived disagreement, and chosen the next experiment together. It should not be the first proof that the project cares.

## Appendix A — The ten-item version

Use this as the first public experiment:

1. Create the Print.World first-believer collection with fixed rules.
2. Verify the chain and exact mint.
3. Publish the creator and project identity card.
4. Explain the bot’s signals, limits, and failure history.
5. Check holder concentration and liquidity.
6. Verify every lock and burn claim.
7. Explain migration and post-migration risks.
8. Publish the community-funding target and refund rule.
9. Ask readers to submit one receipt or falsifier.
10. Decide whether paying the DEX is still the best next action.

## Appendix B — The trust ledger

For every public claim, record:

- claim;
- source URL or transaction;
- source date;
- observation timestamp;
- population and scope;
- what the source supports;
- what it does not support;
- confidence;
- falsifier;
- correction status.

## Appendix C — The first seven-day experiment

Day 1: publish the ten-item checklist and the Print.World collection constitution.

Day 2: publish the identity card and invite corrections.

Day 3: publish the bot transparency card with one false positive.

Day 4: publish the holder/liquidity audit.

Day 5: publish the migration readiness card.

Day 6: publish the community funding alternatives, including the DEX cost and non-DEX options.

Day 7: publish the scoreboard: completed checks, receipts, corrections, unanswered questions, and the decision whether to pay.

Success is not a higher chart. Success is evidence that people can participate in the trust process without being reduced to exit liquidity.
