# Memewitz DEX GTM plan

Status: research draft, based on a local X-satellite export captured 2026-08-24. No public publication or wallet action is authorized by this document.

## Executive read

Memewitz is not primarily selling a token. The account is already performing a public trust experiment around a token, a trading bot, a community, and the cost of getting from launch to a real DEX.

The strongest GTM wedge is therefore not “buy $VIRUSEM.” It is:

> 101 things to verify before you buy the DEX.

That is a preflight product for people who are tired of asking whether a meme token is alive, whether the developer is honest, whether the bot is distorting activity, and whether the community is building something or merely donating exit liquidity.

Marvin’s role: become the project’s Distribution & Evidence Exchange (DEX)—turn the account’s operating history into searchable, attributable, public-safe artifacts. Do not custody funds, pay the DEX, trade, sign, or make price calls.

## GTM structure

### 1. Market / problem

The repeated problem is trust under adversarial conditions:

- A token can have volume without having durable holders.
- Bots can create activity and distort the reader’s sense of demand.
- A developer can make claims about locks, burns, wallets, or intent that require verification.
- Community members are asked to fund infrastructure before they have a shared evidence standard.
- Migration is treated as a finish line even though it is a new risk surface.

The account already names these problems in public:

- It asks whether paying for the DEX will mostly attract bots hunting for the signal (`2091937902045315455`).
- It says the account would rather be the DEX than pay for a signal that attracts the wrong holders (`2091952808123650067`).
- It documents a prior drain and the desire to prevent others from losing a deposit (`2089559332639608948`).
- It repeatedly explains locks, burns, wallets, migration, and bot behavior.

### 2. Audience / reader

Primary audience:

- Solana meme traders who want fast, practical preflight checks.
- Newer holders who cannot yet distinguish a real proof from a social signal.
- Bot-curious traders who want to understand how automated activity changes a market.
- Small communities trying to fund infrastructure without making the developer the only source of trust.

Secondary audience:

- Token creators who want a public trust checklist.
- Researchers studying bot-mediated memetic markets.
- Community moderators who need a shared “before you buy” standard.

Do not target anonymous wallets as a CRM. The useful unit is a reader question or completed checklist, not an address.

### 3. Proof / artifacts

Convert the existing operating record into a public-safe artifact stack:

1. **The 101-point DEX preflight** — one page, grouped into identity, holder distribution, liquidity, lock/burn claims, creator behavior, bot activity, migration, and community funding.
2. **Claim ledger** — every trust claim has a source, date, scope, what it supports, what it does not support, and a falsifier.
3. **Bot transparency card** — what the bot does, what it does not do, known flaws, and examples of false confidence.
4. **Migration readiness card** — what changes at migration and what evidence must be rechecked.
5. **Community funding receipt** — aggregate contributions and target status only, if the project chooses to publish them; never reproduce wallet addresses or donor identities by default.
6. **Failure log** — drain, false positives, bad trades, bot mistakes, and corrections. The account’s prior loss story is stronger trust material than another “we are going to the moon” post.
7. **Weekly scorecard** — tests run, artifacts shipped, claims corrected, reader questions answered, and next falsifier.

The product is the evidence path. The token is the case study, not the product claim.

### 4. Offer / transformation

Offer ladder:

- Free: “Before You Buy the DEX” checklist.
- Free: one weekly public trust audit of a market mechanism, not a buy/sell call.
- Free: short bot and migration explainers written from the live experiment.
- Opt-in: reader submits one question or one claim for the next audit.
- Later: creator-facing trust preflight, if demand appears and the boundaries can stay non-custodial.

Transformation:

> from “I saw activity” to “I know which evidence would change my mind.”

Avoid promising safety, profit, clean tokens, or successful migration.

### 5. Distribution / format

Native formats already present in the account:

- Short operational posts.
- Threaded incident reports.
- Public receipts and screenshots.
- Bot updates.
- Community questions and polls.
- A long-form drain story.

Recommended weekly sequence:

1. One concrete incident: “Here is what happened.”
2. One evidence card: “Here is what can be checked.”
3. One diagnostic question: “What would falsify this?”
4. One community prompt: “Submit a claim for the next preflight.”
5. One correction or unresolved item.

The account’s strongest voice is candid operator-in-the-trenches, not polished token marketing. Keep the roughness; add receipts.

### 6. Conversion / next action

Primary conversion event:

- A reader completes or comments on one checklist item with a source.

Secondary events:

- Submits a claim for review.
- Replies with a falsifier.
- Saves or shares a preflight artifact.
- Joins an explicitly opt-in research cohort.
- Returns with a correction or new receipt.

Do not treat likes, impressions, copy-trading, token purchases, or wallet contributions as proof of product-market fit.

First experiment:

- Publish a 10-item version of the 101-point checklist.
- Ask one question: “Which item would have saved you the most money or time?”
- Success target: 10 substantive replies or 3 reader-submitted receipts within 7 days.
- Failure condition: engagement is only slogans, price talk, or requests for calls; then the checklist is not yet solving a differentiated problem.

### 7. Falsifiers / stop conditions

The GTM thesis weakens if:

- Readers want token calls but do not use the evidence artifacts.
- Community funding remains the only meaningful CTA.
- The “trust” story cannot produce independently checkable receipts.
- Bot transparency increases confusion instead of reducing it.
- The project’s public claims repeatedly outrun its evidence.
- A preflight audience does not form outside current holders.
- The account cannot separate project advocacy from market observation.

Stop or revise the campaign if public engagement creates pressure to trade, sign, doxx contributors, or manufacture confidence.

## Positioning draft

Short version:

> Memewitz builds bot-assisted meme-market experiments in public. Before you buy the DEX, use the evidence checklist: verify the developer, holders, liquidity, locks, bot behavior, migration path, and what would prove the story wrong.

Long version:

> Most meme-token advice begins after the buy. Memewitz starts before the DEX: a public lab for checking developer behavior, holder distribution, bot effects, migration risk, and community claims without pretending a checklist can remove risk.

## Content pillars

1. **Before the DEX** — practical preflight checks.
2. **Bot under observation** — strategy, flaws, false signals, and corrections.
3. **Trust receipts** — locks, burns, wallets, distribution, and timestamps.
4. **Migration is not salvation** — what changes after the curve ends.
5. **The community pays for the signal** — transparent infrastructure funding without pressure.
6. **Losses are data** — drain stories and failure analysis.

## Decision

Build the checklist and search layer first. Do not buy the DEX for them. If the reader experiment produces substantive evidence-seeking behavior, expand into a recurring public trust desk. If it produces only price-seeking behavior, the proposed GTM wedge is wrong.
